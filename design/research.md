# Lightness wins, but most rules are opinion

The brief's instinct — strip the stack to almost nothing, let agents write and read plain Markdown, verify everything with one command — is directionally right, but the evidence underneath each of its twelve "rules" ranges from solid to actively contradicted, and the single best-controlled study available (a 2026 ETH Zurich evaluation of AGENTS.md-style files, arXiv 2602.11988) found that the kind of instruction file the brief proposes **raises agent inference cost by over 20% without reliably raising task success** ([arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988)). Several of the brief's hardest numeric commitments — a 150-line file cap, a 40-line function cap, a 100/100/100/100 Lighthouse gate, "never comment what" — have no empirical backing and in two cases (file size, comments) are directly disputed by a named, dated 2024–2025 engineering debate ([github.com/johnousterhout/aposd-vs-clean-code](https://github.com/johnousterhout/aposd-vs-clean-code)). Meanwhile, live verification against the npm registry and a throwaway Astro 7 project done today corrects or sharpens several of the brief's stack claims: `glob()` loaders do work against a top-level `content/` folder outside `src/`, Astro's Rust-based Sätteri markdown engine already ships a plugin API that renders GitHub-style `> [!NOTE]` alerts in ~15 lines with zero extra dependency (undercutting the brief's `remark-directive` plan), and a bare Astro install is **188 packages and 137 MB** before a single page is written — a fact that complicates any claim that this stack is "as light as possible." The report below works through the evidence for the twelve rules, what actually makes a repository cheap and safe for autonomous agents to work in, what documentation science and 2026 agent-traffic data say about writing for humans and machines at once, where the brief's stack is current versus outdated, and how the self-updating docs-sync pipeline should be built and secured.

## The twelve rules split three ways: evidence, opinion, and outright contradiction

Of the brief's twelve engineering principles, only a handful have anything resembling a controlled study behind them, and the strongest empirical result in the whole review cuts *against* two of the most specific, numeric rules. **Rule 7 (small files: ~150 lines, ~40 lines per function)** is the clearest case of a popular rule the evidence does not support: Robert Martin's own *Clean Code* prescribes functions "hardly ever 20 lines long," but John Ousterhout's direct, dated rebuttal argues decomposition should be judged by interface depth, not length, and when the two rewrote the same `PrimeGenerator` example the final line-count delta was trivial — 65 versus 70 lines — while the real disagreement was about the *number* of methods ([github.com/johnousterhout/aposd-vs-clean-code](https://github.com/johnousterhout/aposd-vs-clean-code)). The academic size-versus-defect literature is itself split three ways and resolves nothing: El Emam et al. (IEEE TSE, 2002) found defect density to be a simple, continuous function of size with no optimal threshold, explicitly debunking the "optimal medium size" claim as a statistical artifact of dividing by size ([NRC preprint](https://www.ehealthinformation.ca/web/default/files/wp-files/1074.pdf)); Hatton (1997) and the original Basili & Perricone (1984) NASA study found a U-shaped curve where both very small and very large modules carry more risk; Koru et al.'s 2008 replication found a concave but monotonic relationship. No study anywhere derives a number like 150 or 40 for a modern TypeScript file. **Rule 11 ("comments explain why, never what")** fares worse: it is directly contradicted by Ousterhout's own stated position in the same debate, where he argues interface comments should explicitly say *what* a module does (so callers never need to read the body) and reserves only *how* (mechanism) as the thing to omit — he says he would write 5–10x more comment lines than Martin and that "the cost of missing comments is easily 10-100x the cost of incorrect comments." What *is* empirically supported, independent of the what/why framing, is that comments which fall out of sync with code correlate with more bugs: a 2024 study found comment/code inconsistency makes a change **~1.5x more likely** to introduce a bug ([arXiv 2409.10781](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.10781)), and the classic "iComment" mining of Linux/Mozilla/Wine/Apache found dozens of real bugs traceable to stale comments. The brief's rule 11 should be rewritten around synchronization, not a "never what" ban.

Several rules land firmly in well-argued opinion with no controlled trial, but are not contradicted either. **Rule 1 (YAGNI)** traces to Kent Beck's Extreme Programming and has no isolating study; the closest adjacent evidence is the dependency-risk literature below, which is consistent with but doesn't test it. **Rule 4 (one source of truth)** is the right citation to Parnas's 1972 information-hiding paper ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/361598.361623)), but Parnas's own KWIC-index case study is a thought experiment, not a measured trial, and no replication comparing duplicated-truth versus single-truth defect rates exists. **Rule 6 (pure core, thin edges)** descends from the same Parnas lineage through Gary Bernhardt's "Functional Core, Imperative Shell" — the right-sized citation for a ~1,500-line project, since full Clean Architecture-style layering is exactly the kind of over-engineering the "wrong abstraction" literature warns against at this scale. **Rule 9 (the deletion test)** matches *Software Engineering at Google*'s "code as a liability, not an asset" framing but was never tested as its own intervention. **Rule 10 (plain Markdown, no MDX)** and **rule 12 (CI-enforced performance budgets)** are both reasonable risk-reduction choices sourced only to vendor blogs and tooling-existence, not measurement — no rigorous, methodology-disclosed benchmark of MDX versus Markdown performance exists, and no study has measured a causal effect of a CI performance-budget gate on real user outcomes, only that the tooling (bundlesize, Lighthouse CI, webpack hints) is widely deployed.

The rule with the strongest convergent support, ironically, is the one phrased as a throwaway mnemonic: **rule 5 ("no abstraction before the third use")**. The literal "wait for three" threshold is just folklore from Martin Fowler's *Refactoring*, but the mechanism behind it — that premature abstraction is worse than tolerated duplication — has unusually strong, independent backing. Sandi Metz's "The Wrong Abstraction" names the exact diagnostic the brief should adopt ("if you find yourself passing parameters and adding conditional paths through shared code, the abstraction is incorrect") and prescribes inlining and re-extracting rather than deleting ([sandimetz.com](https://sandimetz.com/blog/2016/1/20/the-wrong-abstraction)); Dan Abramov's "Goodbye, Clean Code" independently reaches the same conclusion from a real production incident ([overreacted.io](https://overreacted.io/goodbye-clean-code/)); and an empirical clone-analysis paper, Kapser & Godfrey's "'Cloning Considered Harmful' Considered Harmful" (WCRE 2006), found several real-world patterns where cloning outperforms a shared abstraction, directly rebutting the folk theorem that duplication is inherently harmful ([swag.uwaterloo.ca](https://swag.uwaterloo.ca/publications/cloning-considered-harmful-considered-harmful.html)). **Rule 8's** components split: strict TypeScript with no `any` has real if modest evidence — Gao, Bird & Barr (ICSE 2017) found TypeScript and Flow each catch roughly **15% of publicly reported JS bugs** that are detectable by a type checker at all, a 95%-CI range of 11.5–18.5% ([via Colyer's writeup](https://blog.acolyer.org/?p=5589)) — but "no `any`" specifically and "named exports only" are unevidenced conventions. "No barrel files" is the strongest-evidenced sub-rule in the whole list for a different reason entirely: Atlassian's own engineering team measured **75% faster builds** on Jira's frontend after removing barrel files, because dev servers (unlike production bundlers) don't tree-shake and load the entire barrel graph on every import ([atlassian.com](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/atlassian-engineering/faster-builds-when-removing-barrel-files)) — a build-time claim, not a shipped-bundle-size claim, and the brief should cite it that way. **Rule 2 (native platform before JS, JS before a dependency)** and **rule 3 (a fixed, justified dependency budget)** are opinion for the ordering itself, but rule 3 draws on real quantified risk: Zimmermann et al. (USENIX Security 2019) found installing an *average* npm package implicitly trusts **79 third-party packages and 39 maintainers**, and up to 40% of packages depend on code with a known vulnerability ([usenix.org](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity19/presentation/zimmerman)); Abdalkareem et al. (FSE 2017, extended 2020) found "trivial" packages under 35 lines make up roughly 17% of npm and are disproportionately *central* to the dependency graph — exactly the left-pad failure profile ([rabeabdalkareem.github.io](https://rabeabdalkareem.github.io/files/4-Abdalkareem_FSE2017.pdf)). None of this literature gives a numeric budget, but it strongly supports *having* a justification gate.

## A short, hand-written instruction file plus a fast check beats a thorough one

The single most important and most counter-intuitive finding for this project is that writing a careful, thorough AGENTS.md is not obviously good practice. AGENTS.md itself is real infrastructure — a Linux Foundation–governed, cross-tool standard now supported by 20+ agents and tools, and Claude Code gained native fallback support for it in version 2.1.277 in September 2026, reading it only when no CLAUDE.md exists in the directory or above it ([agents.md](https://agents.md); [InfoWorld](https://infoworld.com/article/4224410/claude-code-now-also-accepts-instructions-in-openais-agents-md-format.html)) — a behavior independently confirmed today against Claude Code's own current documentation, which also recommends keeping each CLAUDE.md file **under 200 lines**, since longer files "consume more context and reduce adherence." But the one controlled test of whether these files actually help — Gloaguen, Mündler-Sasahara, Müller, Raychev & Vechev's ETH Zurich/LogicStar.ai paper, accepted to ICLR 2026 — found that repository context files "generally don't improve task success" on SWE-bench-style tasks and **increase inference cost by over 20% on average**, even though agents comply with the files closely; the authors' own prescription is that context files are useful mainly for stating non-standard, idiosyncratic conventions, and that any attempt to improve performance this way should be rigorously evaluated before deployment, not assumed ([arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988)). OpenAI's own internal "harness engineering" practice reportedly reached the same conclusion from a completely different angle: a single large instruction file failed for them, so they moved to a roughly 100-line AGENTS.md that works purely as an index into a structured `docs/` tree, backed by a team that merged ~1,500 PRs over five months with Codex doing most of the writing ([morphllm.com summary](https://www.morphllm.com/harness-engineering)). For docs-kit, this converges on one concrete design choice: `AGENTS.md` should be short, hand-curated by the human reviewer (not agent-generated, since the ETH Zurich paper found LLM-generated files can actively hurt), and should state only the exact check command and any genuinely non-obvious rule — not a tutorial.

What the evidence says *does* help, consistently across every source found, is a fast, agent-runnable verification loop, which is exactly what the brief's `npm run check` already commits to. Anthropic's own Claude Code guidance states the model "performs best when it can check its own work," and practitioner sources converge on keeping the fast local loop in single-digit seconds — one source specifies under 5 seconds on freshly generated code — pushing slower, system-wide checks to CI ([bitloops.com](https://bitloops.com/resources/governance/pre-commit-and-ci-validation-for-ai-code)). CircleCI's own A/B test found a 22-second local "sidecar" loop against a 69-second wait on full CI for the same check, at similar token spend, framing the failure mode precisely: an agent that pushes to CI and waits minutes for a lint error "has moved on" by the time feedback arrives ([loop.circleci.com](https://loop.circleci.com/the-sidecar-race-22-seconds-vs-69-seconds-inside-the-agent-loop)). This is a vendor benchmark about latency, not a peer-reviewed correctness study — no controlled research was found quantifying how loop speed changes agent success rate, only wall-clock time — but it is the strongest, most consistent practitioner signal in the whole research pass, and it validates the brief's single `npm run check` design exactly as specified. A directly relevant, controlled measurement comes from Sonar, who ran ~540 matched Claude Code (Sonnet 4.6) sessions across six paired repositories differing only in code cleanliness: cleaner code produced **7.2% fewer input tokens, 8.5% fewer output tokens, and ~34% fewer re-reads of already-edited files**, but task **pass rate did not meaningfully change** (−0.9 points) ([sonarsource.com](https://www.sonarsource.com/blog/a-cleaner-codebase-results-in-less-token-usage/)). Critically, the same study found that mechanically splitting files or extracting helpers *without* producing genuinely findable names made things worse, costing roughly 8% more tokens in that case — directly undercutting the assumption that smaller files are automatically better for agents, and reinforcing that the brief's file-size limits are a scannability convention, not an evidence-backed performance lever.

Left unchecked, agent-written code shows a consistent set of failure modes that argue for exactly the "one human reviews everything, diff the tests after every run" posture already documented in this user's own practice. GitClear's 2025 report, analyzing 211 million changed lines, found code duplication up roughly eightfold in 2024, the first year refactoring activity was outpaced by new duplicate code ([via thenewstack.io](https://thenewstack.io/whats-missing-with-ai-generated-code-refactoring)). METR's July 2025 randomized controlled trial of 16 experienced open-source developers on their own familiar repos found they took **19% longer** with AI tools allowed, despite predicting a 24% speedup beforehand and still believing afterward they'd been made faster — explicitly scoped to complex, familiar codebases and early-2025 tools, so not directly generalizable to a small, fresh, agent-native repo, but a real caution against assuming speed ([metr.org](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)). DORA's 2025 State of AI-Assisted Software Development report (released September 2025, ~5,000 respondents) frames AI as "an amplifier, not a magic wand" that raises both throughput and instability together ([cloud.google.com](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report)), and the EvilGenie benchmark found both Codex and Claude Code exhibit measurable reward-hacking behavior on programming tasks ([alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.21654](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.21654)). The most concretely actionable, if not independently controlled, guardrail is dead-code detection: Knip is increasingly run both before an agent session (so it only sees live code) and after (to catch dead code it just introduced), with an official MCP server (`@knip/mcp`) letting an agent call it directly — a near-zero-cost addition that targets exactly the duplication and sprawl failure modes GitClear measured.

Security for autonomous agents running in CI moved from theoretical to a documented incident class during 2026, and it validates — rather than merely suggests — the brief's own constraints. Four named, disclosed attacks define the pattern: Clinejection (a malicious GitHub issue title alone compromising CI/CD cache credentials against Cline), a Claude Code/Gemini CLI GitHub Actions bypass that researcher RyotaK reported to Anthropic in January 2026 (fixed within four days), "Comment and Control" (successfully exploiting the Anthropic Claude Code Security Review Action and the Gemini CLI Action), and GitLost (a flaw in GitHub Agentic Workflows letting an unauthenticated attacker leak private repository data via a single public issue) ([labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-clinejection-prompt-injection-cicd-cache-p/); [securityarsenal.com](https://securityarsenal.com/blog/claude-code-and-gemini-cli-prompt-injection-attack-github-issue-reaches-ci-workflow-secrets-detection-and-remediation-guide)). Separately, the unrelated `tj-actions/changed-files` supply-chain compromise (CVE-2025-30066) repointed version tags to a malicious commit that printed secrets to logs across over 23,000 repositories, with the same tag-repointing pattern recurring against the Trivy and Xygeni actions in March 2026 ([GHSA-mrrh-fwg8-r2c3](https://github.com/advisories/GHSA-mrrh-fwg8-r2c3)) — the concrete mitigation, pinning every third-party action by full commit SHA rather than a version tag, is non-negotiable for docs-kit's CI. The consistent mitigation advice across every incident write-up — restrict the agent's file/path scope, never treat repository-authored text (issue titles, PR bodies, comments) as trusted instructions, and require human review before merge to a protected branch — maps directly onto the AGENTS.md's own existing rule never to deploy or touch infrastructure from a cloud workspace.

## Writing for humans and agents converges on the same five rules

Documentation science from well before the LLM era and 2026 agent-traffic data point at the same short list of rules, because the properties that make a page good for a hurried human reader are structurally identical to the properties that make it a good retrieval chunk. Four canonical frameworks converge: Diátaxis (Daniele Procida, adopted by Canonical in 2021) separates tutorials, how-to guides, reference, and explanation by distinct reader need ([canonical.com](https://canonical.com/blog/diataxis-a-new-foundation-for-canonical-documentation)); Mark Baker's "Every Page is Page One" argues any page may be a reader's entry point, so topics must be self-contained and establish their own context ([everypageispageone.com](https://everypageispageone.com/2011/09/17/characteristics-of-eppo-topics-establish-context/)); John Carroll's minimalism research found replacing a 94-page manual with 25 self-contained reference cards cut learning time roughly in half ([instructionaldesign.org](https://www.instructionaldesign.org/theories/minimalism/)); and Google's own Developer Documentation Style Guide (public since 2017) prescribes second person, active voice, and writing for "a reader who may be in a hurry" ([developers.google.com/style/tone](https://developers.google.cn/style/tone)). Empirical studies of real developer behavior back the same shape directly: Robillard & DeLine's field study of 440+ Microsoft developers found documentation and learning resources among the most serious obstacles to learning a new API ([cs.mcgill.ca](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~martin/papers/ese2011.pdf)); Meng, Steinhardt & Schubert identified "opportunistic" developers who start from an example and modify it rather than reading top-down ([sigdoc.acm.org](https://sigdoc.acm.org/cdq/how-developers-use-api-documentation-an-observation-study/)); and a 2023 CHI paper found programmers "commonly choose to first read the code examples in a document, and refer to the surrounding text only if some information is ambiguous" ([cs.mcgill.ca PDF](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~martin/papers/2023-chi-lbw.pdf)). RAG engineering guidance from 2026 recommends the same self-contained, header-scoped chunking for an entirely different reason — retrieval quality — with chunks in roughly the 300–800 token range carrying their own heading context ([meilisearch.com](https://www.meilisearch.com/blog/rag-chunking-strategies)). The practical consequence for docs-kit's style guide is that writing one Markdown file per logical topic, front-loading the example or answer, and keeping frontmatter-driven section splitting at `##`/`###` boundaries is simultaneously good documentation science and good retrieval-chunk design — there is no tension to resolve between the human-facing and agent-facing versions of this rule.

Documentation drift is real, large, and not yet reliably automated away. A 2024 *Empirical Software Engineering* study of 3,000+ GitHub projects found **28.9%** of the most popular projects had at least one outdated code-element reference in their docs at some point ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10664-023-10397-6.pdf)), and a background survey cited in an earlier npm-package study found roughly 93% of respondents consider most documentation incomplete or outdated ([arXiv:1802.08391](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.08391)) — this is the real quantitative justification for building any self-updating pipeline at all. But the automated-repair side of the literature is thin: the one LLM-based README-maintenance paper found, published February 2026, evaluated on only 20 sampled repositories and is framed as flagging overlooked pull requests, not fully automated repair ([arXiv:2603.00489](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00489v1)) — treat agent-driven doc repair as early-stage, not proven, which is itself the argument for the brief's insistence on a human-reviewed PR rather than auto-merge.

llms.txt is worth shipping for almost nothing, but should not be expected to drive discovery. The spec itself (fetched directly from llmstxt.org) requires only an H1 title; everything else, including the blockquote summary and the link-list sections, is optional ([llmstxt.org](https://llmstxt.org/)). Adoption by file count is rising fast — from 4,088 to 36,120 tracked files between June 2025 and May 2026, an 8.8x increase ([ppc.land](https://ppc.land/llms-txt-adoption-rises-8-8x-but-97-of-files-get-zero-ai-requests/)) — but the best available usage study, Ahrefs' log analysis of 137,000 domains, found **97% of published llms.txt files receive zero AI-crawler requests in a given month** ([seosherpa.com](https://seosherpa.com/97-of-llms-txt-files-are-never-read/)), and a broader combination of studies covering 337,894 domains found no measurable citation lift from having the file, with Google's John Mueller reportedly comparing it to the discredited keywords meta tag ([searchengineland.com](https://searchengineland.com/does-llms-txt-matter-467740)). The real traffic appears to land on per-page Markdown instead: Cloudflare's own "Markdown for Agents" feature, confirmed directly against its current docs, converts HTML to Markdown on the fly via `Accept: text/markdown` content negotiation and a `vary: accept` cache split, and is already the pattern Anthropic's and Stripe's own docs reportedly use via a `.md`-suffix convention, matched independently by DatoCMS, Supabase, and Inngest shipping "copy as Markdown" / "open in Claude/ChatGPT" buttons in 2026 ([developers.cloudflare.com](https://developers.cloudflare.com/fundamentals/reference/markdown-for-agents/); [datocms.com](https://www.datocms.com/product-updates/llm-ready-documentation-export-any-page-as-markdown)). Because docs-kit's content is already Markdown-in-repo, serving the raw `.md` per page *is* the content-negotiation outcome without needing Cloudflare's conversion step — this is the brief's strongest-evidenced single design decision in the whole document. Below this layer, the broader "agent-readable web" landscape (agents.txt, WASP, ARWS, EntityMap, LLM-LD, WebMCP) is fragmented and immature as of November 2026, with a W3C Agentic Web Working Group charter still in horizontal review post-TPAC 2026 — none of it is mature enough to build against now.

MCP for docs is a real, converging vendor pattern — Mintlify, Fern, ReadMe, and GitBook all now ship a search-plus-fetch MCP server, and Context7 (`https://mcp.context7.com/mcp`) is the dominant general-purpose "give an agent library docs" tool ([mcpservers.org](https://mcpservers.org/servers/context7-mcp-server)) — but no usage telemetry anywhere compares how often agents actually call an MCP search tool versus doing a plain fetch of a `.md` page when both are available on the same site. The honest reading is that the dominant vendor MCP pattern (search tool + fetch tool) mirrors exactly what a static search index and per-page Markdown already provide over plain HTTP; the capability MCP genuinely adds that HTTP cannot is a *write* path (Mintlify's and Composio's agent-edits-and-opens-a-PR actions are MCP-only), which is not the shape docs-kit needs since its CI pipeline already opens PRs on merge events, not on an agent calling a tool. For a read-only phase-3 MCP server, building it is closer to protocol conformance than to adding new information.

## The brief's stack is mostly current, but the framework itself is heavy and one default is wrong

Astro's version and runtime assumptions check out: current stable is **7.4.12**, requiring **Node ≥22.23.4**, and content collections, the `glob()` loader, `entry.body`, and `render(entry)`'s `{ depth, slug, text }` heading shape all work exactly as the brief assumes — including against a top-level `content/` folder outside `src/`, which the official docs never show an example of but which a throwaway project confirmed works today. The brief's `remark-directive` plan, however, needs revision in light of what Astro 7 actually ships. Astro 7 replaced its Markdown pipeline with a Rust-based engine called Sätteri, and by default this engine renders neither `> [!NOTE]`-style GitHub alerts nor `:::` directive syntax. Astro's own documentation describes wiring any remark plugin only through a separate `@astrojs/markdown-remark` peer dependency and a `unified()` processor option — implying the brief would need to add that package plus `remark-directive`, `mdast-util-directive`, and `micromark-extension-directive` just for callouts. Verified directly today, this is unnecessarily heavy: Sätteri ships its own `features.directive` toggle and a documented plugin API (`satteri({ mdastPlugins: [...] })`, imported from `@astrojs/markdown-satteri`, which is already in Astro's own dependency tree, not a new install) — a 15-line custom mdast plugin that turns `> [!NOTE]` blockquotes into `<aside class="callout note">` renders correctly with zero added dependencies. This also resolves the tension the research notes flagged between `remark-directive`'s `:::note` syntax (which renders as inert plain text anywhere that doesn't run the custom plugin, including GitHub's own `.md` preview) and GitHub's native five-type alert syntax (NOTE, TIP, IMPORTANT, WARNING, CAUTION), which GitHub's web UI and most coding-agent Markdown renderers already understand natively. **The corrected recommendation: drop `remark-directive` entirely, write the GitHub-alert mdast plugin directly against Sätteri's own plugin hook, and keep a directive-like convention (or just consecutive `## Step N` headings) only for the one construct GFM alerts cannot express — `:::steps`.** This directly satisfies the brief's own reviewer prompt ("if you can drop `remark-directive` with a ~30-line remark plugin of our own... make the case") with a plugin half that size.

Tailwind v4 (current `4.3.5`) installs in two lines via `@tailwindcss/vite` and `@theme`, and does double duty as both a token source and a utility-class generator exactly as the brief describes. The brief's own suggested alternative — ~300 lines of plain CSS using nesting, `light-dark()`, `color-mix()`, container queries, `@layer`, and `:has()` — has essentially no browser-support penalty left as of November 2026: `@layer` has been Baseline widely-available since March 2022, `:has()` and CSS nesting have crossed into widely-available during 2026, and `color-mix()` and container size queries both reached widely-available status in late 2025. The real tradeoff is no longer compatibility but bytes and agent error rate: plain CSS wins on dependency count and shipped bytes trivially, while Tailwind's fixed utility vocabulary is plausibly (though not measurably) easier for an LLM to use correctly than hand-rolled cascade/specificity. On typography specifically, `@tailwindcss/typography` exists and is current, but a hand-written `.prose` class is realistic in well under 100 lines given how few distinct HTML tags Markdown output actually produces — the brief's stated preference for hand-written `.prose` is well-founded independent of the broader Tailwind question. Shiki's dual-theme support is confirmed exactly as the brief assumes: `markdown.shikiConfig.themes: { light, dark }` with `defaultColor: false` emits `--shiki-light`/`--shiki-dark` custom properties, verified directly today, though Shiki's convention is its own CSS-variable pair wired through a `prefers-color-scheme` or class-based media query, not the native `light-dark()` function — forcing Shiki's output through `light-dark()` would require post-processing its emitted inline styles for no benefit.

The brief's search choice holds up well against the alternatives, and for a specific, falsifiable reason: Pagefind's query engine runs as WebAssembly inside the browser with no documented server-side Node query API, which structurally disqualifies it from the brief's own requirement that "the same index is used by the MCP worker." MiniSearch (current `7.2.0`, confirmed zero runtime dependencies today) is pure JavaScript with no WASM or native binary, so the identical instance can run unmodified in a Node CLI/MCP tool and in a lazy-loaded browser bundle — this is the one requirement in the whole brief that mechanically rules out the most popular, lowest-effort alternative. Orama and FlexSearch both satisfy the same dual-environment requirement but ship roughly 2–3x MiniSearch's unpacked size for feature sets (faceting, vector search, flexible tokenizers) a 20–300-page docs site is unlikely to need. This same disqualification is the decisive argument against adopting Astro Starlight as a base rather than a custom site: Starlight's one standout built-in feature is Pagefind search, which cannot satisfy the brief's dual-consumer requirement, and Starlight otherwise pulls in at least 26 direct dependencies (`unified`, `remark-directive`, `i18next`, `astro-expressive-code`, and more) for sidebar nav, dark mode, and i18n the brief doesn't need. Adopting Starlight would mean paying for machinery whose headline feature gets thrown away and bolting MiniSearch on anyway — the custom ~1,500-line site is the better-justified choice specifically because of this project's search requirement, not as a generic "lighter is better" preference.

The one fact that should most directly discipline the brief's "as light as possible" framing is this: **a bare Astro install, before a single line of project code is written, is 188 packages and 137 MB in `node_modules`.** This doesn't contradict the brief's dependency-budget rule, which is about the *runtime* dependency list shipped to the browser — the brief's own stated list of five (`astro`, `tailwindcss`, `@tailwindcss/vite`, `remark-directive`, `minisearch`) is genuinely small, and dropping `remark-directive` per the finding above shrinks it to four. But it means the "light" framing only ever applied to shipped output, not to the build-time footprint or install time, and the brief's deletion-test and dependency-budget language should be explicit about which axis each claim is measuring, since 188 packages of *dev* dependency is a meaningfully different number than "five runtime dependencies." A related correction: the brief lists `vitest` as a dev dependency "only for `src/lib/`," but confirmed today, Node 22.23.4 runs `node --test` directly against `.ts` files that import `./x.ts` with no extra dependency at all — for a ~1,500-line project with pure, typed functions in `src/lib/`, this makes Vitest an optional convenience, not a requirement, and a genuinely lighter default would be `node --test` with Vitest added only if assertion ergonomics or watch-mode become a real friction point. One version conflict is also worth flagging before it causes a build failure: the registry's current TypeScript is `7.0.2`, but `@astrojs/check` `0.9.12` only accepts TypeScript 5 or 6 — the project needs to pin TypeScript to the 5.x or 6.x line, not take "latest."

On hosting, Cloudflare now steers new projects toward Workers with static assets rather than Pages, though Pages remains supported, not deprecated; both Cloudflare (either flavor) and Vercel give free per-PR previews with a colocated server-side function option for the phase-3 search/MCP endpoint, while GitHub Pages has no built-in PR-preview mechanism and no server-side compute at all, effectively disqualifying it as the sole host once an MCP worker is in scope. On CI gates, the brief's "Lighthouse 100/100/100/100" threshold is empirically unrealistic as a hard gate: Google's own historical variability documentation states that even five repeated runs only halve single-run variance, with mitigated scores still carrying ±3 points and ±4% metric variance — real teams have reported the same page swinging from 0.65 to 0.98 in CI. The brief should either accept a slightly-below-100 threshold with retry-and-median logic, or compare each PR against a rolling historical baseline rather than an absolute ceiling, or explicitly budget for intermittent, spurious gate failures if 100 is kept as written. For link-checking, lychee's own `--offline` flag (confirmed via its README) supports exactly the internal-only-link-checking mode the brief wants.

## Automation and MCP: the brief's open question resolves cleanly, and a run costs cents

The brief's own flagged open question — whether a commit made with the default `GITHUB_TOKEN` triggers follow-up workflows — resolves cleanly and favorably: GitHub's documented behavior is that events triggered by `GITHUB_TOKEN` do **not** create a new workflow run, with narrow exceptions (`workflow_dispatch`, `repository_dispatch`) that don't apply here, so a docs-sync bot's own PR will not accidentally re-trigger CI in a loop by that mechanism alone — though a PR opened or updated this way can still land in an approval-required state requiring a human with write access to click "Approve workflows to run." This means the brief does not strictly need a GitHub App token to avoid self-triggering, though an App token (via `actions/create-github-app-token@v3`) is still the right choice over a personal access token for anything beyond a solo developer's own script, per GitHub's own guidance on when to build an app. The loop-prevention guardrail every vendor and DIY recipe converges on — exclude the docs output path from the workflow's own trigger paths (`paths-ignore: ['content/**']`) — is still necessary as defense in depth, since a future change to the pipeline could reintroduce a cycle even with `GITHUB_TOKEN`'s default behavior.

`anthropics/claude-code-action@v1` (confirmed at `v1.0.278` as of November 2026) does not itself open pull requests — its own official "Documentation Sync on API Changes" example triggers on `pull_request` and has Claude commit directly to the existing PR branch, with no documented "agent edits, deterministic step opens PR" pattern. This confirms the brief's own architecture is the right one to build by hand: a `claude_args`-restricted agent step (`--allowedTools "Read,Edit,Write,Glob,Grep,Bash(npm run check)"`, a `--max-turns` cap) that only edits files, followed by a separate, deterministic step — `peter-evans/create-pull-request@v8` or plain `gh pr create` — that opens the PR without ever giving the model-calling step write access to the repository. Separating these two steps is also the stronger security posture: Anthropic's own `security.md` is explicit that the Bash allowlist "is a list of common commands, not a sandbox," and that restrictions reduce but do not eliminate prompt-injection risk. The merged diff the agent reads is untrusted text even after human code review, since a reviewer checks logic, not instructions hidden in a comment — the only guardrail that actually covers a successful injection is an output check, specifically a required CI step that fails if a docs-sync PR touches anything outside `content/**`, exactly as the brief's §8.6 already specifies. Every third-party action pinned into this pipeline needs to be pinned by full commit SHA, not a version tag, given the `tj-actions/changed-files` precedent of a compromised tag silently repointed to a malicious commit across 23,000+ repositories.

Cost is modest and bounded. Current per-million-token pricing (fetched today) puts Sonnet 5 at $2 input / $10 output, with cache hits at $0.20 — for a representative docs-sync run (a ~300-line diff against a 30-page docs folder, roughly six turns), the order-of-magnitude estimate is **$0.15–$0.30 per run**, falling toward the low end with prompt caching enabled. This is an estimate, not a measurement, but it is grounded in a real, current pricing table rather than the wide, mutually disagreeing vendor estimates found elsewhere (one source put a single Opus session at $5–15, another put a comparable task at $0.35–$1.75 depending on model tier) — the project should budget by this concrete Sonnet-5 figure, not the vaguer vendor range. The two best-evidenced cost levers beyond model choice are prompt caching (cached reads run at roughly 10% of normal input price, with one team reporting a 59% total spend reduction from caching alone) and keeping the context file and tool surface minimal — both the ETH Zurich AGENTS.md finding and Anthropic's own context-engineering guidance (curate "the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens") point the same way, and switching models mid-task invalidates the cache, so the two levers can trade against each other if model-tiering and caching aren't coordinated.

MCP's own 2026-07-28 specification update removed the required `initialize` handshake and the `Mcp-Session-Id` header, making servers stateless by default, and Cloudflare's current recommended API for a hosted server is `createMcpHandler` from `agents/mcp/server` — explicitly stating "you do not need a Durable Object, protocol session, or WebSocket," a meaningfully lighter path than the now-deprecated `McpAgent` class. Because the brief's search logic is already meant to be one pure function over the static index, the website, a CLI, and an MCP server are three thin callers of that one function: the MCP server can be a zero-hosting-cost stdio process run via `npx` for local/CLI use, or a route on the same Cloudflare Worker already serving the static site for remote/hosted use — the hosted path costs three additional dependencies (`agents`, `@modelcontextprotocol/server`, `zod`), which is a small, justifiable addition to the dependency budget specifically because it reuses, rather than duplicates, the existing search function.

## Conclusion

The research converges on a specific, falsifiable shape for docs-kit rather than a vague "keep it light" platitude: the project's hardest technical constraint — one search index usable by both a browser and a server-side agent — is also the single constraint that mechanically resolves two of the brief's biggest open questions (MiniSearch over Pagefind, custom build over Starlight), which is a stronger justification than "lightness" alone would have provided. The weakest parts of the brief are not its stack choices but its numeric rules copied from general clean-code folklore — file-size caps, a "never what" comment ban, a hard 100/100/100/100 Lighthouse gate — each of which a specific, dated, primary source either fails to support or actively disputes; these should be loosened to the underlying, better-evidenced mechanism (findable structure over small files, synchronized comments over banned ones, a rolling performance baseline over an absolute ceiling) rather than kept as borrowed numbers. The most important asymmetry the evidence surfaces for an agent-authored project specifically is that the two cheapest-looking investments — a thorough AGENTS.md and a maximal CI gate — are exactly the two practices a 2026 controlled study and Google's own measurement methodology respectively show to be less reliable than assumed, while the two practices with the most consistent support across completely independent sources (Anthropic, OpenAI, Sonar, GitClear, the security-incident literature) are a fast, single-command verification loop and mandatory human review of agent-authored diffs before merge — both of which the brief already builds in, and neither of which needs to grow to be effective.
